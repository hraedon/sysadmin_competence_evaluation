# Domain 16: Observability & Alert Design

*An alert is a contract with a responder*

## Scope and Boundary

This domain owns the design and operation of the alerting and monitoring lifecycle: what to alert on, how to route it, how to keep signal-to-noise sustainable over time, and how to map detection coverage against failure and attack paths rather than compliance checklists.

The map has been circling this topic across multiple domains without giving it a home. Domain 8 (Security Reasoning) identified detection as "the consistently underthin control" — the layer that turns a successful breach into a detectable incident rather than a prolonged, silent compromise. Domain 10 (Backup, Recovery & Resilience) showed alert fatigue destroying the backup monitoring signal: ninety unread failure notifications, each processed as noise. Domain 11 (Log Reading & Diagnosis) showed that logs without alerting are detective, not corrective. Domain 4 (Certificate & PKI Management) showed certificate expiries that monitoring didn't catch. The thread is always the same: the technical control existed, the failure was in the layer that was supposed to notice. This domain takes that thread and makes it a domain.

The boundary with Domain 11 (Log Reading & Diagnosis) is the most important and requires explicit reconciliation. Domain 11 owns the mechanics of reading logs — finding relevant information in real log data, correlating timestamps, distinguishing symptom from cause. Its Level 4 (Detection Design) owns the logging infrastructure decisions: what to collect, how long to retain it, what collection architecture makes evidence available for investigation. This domain owns the layer above that: given that evidence exists, what do we alert on, who gets paged, and will they act? The clean split: Domain 11 (Log Reading & Diagnosis) at Level 4 answers "will the evidence exist when we need it?" This domain's Level 4 answers "will someone know to look, and will they act?" Both are needed; neither substitutes for the other. A logging infrastructure that retains everything but alerts on nothing produces evidence for post-incident investigation but no incident detection. An alerting system that fires on everything but reaches no one produces noise but no response.

The boundary with Domain 8 (Security Reasoning) is also explicit: it owns the security reasoning about what to look for and why — what attacker behavior looks like in infrastructure terms, what an indicator of compromise means conceptually. This domain owns the operational machinery that turns that reasoning into alerts that reach the right person at the right time. Domain 8 (Security Reasoning) says "this pattern matters." This domain says "this pattern triggers this alert, routed to this person, with this escalation path."

Domain 10 (Backup, Recovery & Resilience) owns backup and restore mechanics. This domain owns the monitoring of backups — and the lesson from Domain 10 that backup monitoring is the canonical example of alert fatigue is foundational here, not a cross-reference. The backup success email that became noise is the case study this domain builds on.

*The primary gap this domain addresses is not knowledge of monitoring tools — candidates who have worked with any monitoring stack know the mechanics of configuring alerts. The gap is the ability to design alerting as a system with a lifecycle, a signal-to-noise budget, and a contract with responders that holds over time rather than degrading into noise.*

## Reasoning Framework: An Alert Is a Contract with a Responder

How it is normally taught: monitoring is presented as "set up alerts for things that go wrong." Configure thresholds — CPU above 90%, disk above 80%, error count above N — point them at an email address or a Slack channel, and you are monitoring. The implicit model is that monitoring is a coverage question: do we have alerts for our systems? If every system has alerts, monitoring is complete.

What it is actually for: every alert is a contract with three clauses. First, this signal is worth a human's attention right now — not eventually, not in the morning, now. Second, there is an owner who will respond — a specific person or role, not "the team" or a shared mailbox. Third, the response is defined or at least understood — the responder knows what action the alert implies, even if the action is "investigate and determine what to do." An alert without an owner is a contract with no counterparty. An alert with an owner who has stopped reading is a broken contract. Alert volume that exceeds the responder's capacity to triage produces the same outcome as no alerting — the signal is buried in noise, and the responder's heuristic becomes "ignore until someone complains." The question is never "do we have alerts." It is: when this alert fires, will someone act, and will they act in time?

What misuse looks like: treating monitoring as a checklist of configured alerts rather than a contract with responders. The organization that has alerts on everything and responds to nothing has the same detection capability as the organization with no alerts — but with more operational overhead and a false sense of coverage. This is the Domain 10 (Backup, Recovery & Resilience) lesson generalized: the backup success emails that became noise were alerts that broke the contract. The organization had coverage. It did not have detection. The most dangerous monitoring posture is not one with too few alerts. It is one where the contract has been silently broken — alerts fire, nobody reads them, and the monitoring dashboard shows green because the dashboard reports alert configuration, not alert response.

*The contract framework makes the same move as the risk management framework in Domain 8 (Security Reasoning): it converts an implicit decision into an explicit one. Risk management asks "who accepted this risk?" The contract framework asks "who committed to responding to this alert?" In both cases, the failure mode is the same — decisions made by default, through accumulated inertia, without anyone consciously owning them. An alert that fires into a void is the monitoring equivalent of an implicit risk acceptance: nobody decided the alert didn't matter, but nobody decided it did either.*

### Case Study: The Contract That Broke Silently

An organization deploys a monitoring stack with good intentions. Every server gets CPU, memory, and disk alerts. Every service gets a health check. The database gets connection-count monitoring. Certificates get expiry alerts. The on-call rotation receives pages; the operations channel receives notifications. At deployment, the signal-to-noise ratio is good — twenty alerts a day, most actionable, the contract holds.

Over eighteen months, the environment grows. New services are deployed, each with its own alert rules. Old services are decommissioned, but their alert rules are never removed. Thresholds set for a smaller environment fire more frequently under higher load. The operations channel now receives 150 alerts a day. The on-call rotation receives 40 pages a day. Nobody added staff. Nobody reviewed the alerting rules after initial deployment.

The responders develop coping heuristics. The operations Slack channel is muted — "I'll check it if something seems wrong." Pages are acked reflexively without investigation — "probably another false alarm." The junior team member who checks the backup email mailbox has stopped reading the subject lines. These are rational responses to an unsustainable signal volume. They are also contract violations — the alerts are firing, the contract is being invoked, and the counterparty has stopped performing.

Then a real incident occurs. A database failover leaves the primary in a read-only state. The application continues to function — reads succeed, writes fail silently. The monitoring shows the database is up and accepting connections. The health check passes. The alert that would have caught the problem — a write-operation success check — was never configured because the original monitoring design watched for the database being down, not for the database being read-only. The alert that did fire — elevated connection latency — was one of 150 Slack notifications that day and was not read until the incident was discovered by users four hours later.

The post-incident review discovers that the latency alert fired 47 minutes before the first user complaint. It was buried in noise. The monitoring system detected the problem. The contract was broken — not by the monitoring configuration, but by the accumulated degradation of signal-to-noise that made the signal invisible. The root cause was not a missing alert. It was a broken contract.

*The lesson: an alerting system is not a configuration. It is a relationship between a signal and a responder. Relationships require maintenance. The monitoring system that is deployed and never revisited is the same as the backup that is configured and never tested — it appears to work until it doesn't, and the gap between appearance and reality is discovered during the incident it was supposed to prevent.*

## Core Concepts

### The Three States of Monitoring: Green, Red, and Silent

Every monitored system exists in one of three states. Green: healthy. Monitoring confirms expected behavior and the system is operating within parameters. Red: alerting. Something is wrong, the alert is firing, and the contract is being invoked — someone should be responding. Silent: unknown. The monitoring system is not reporting, and the absence of signal is itself the signal.

The silent state is the most dangerous because it looks like green. The backup that stopped running and stopped sending emails. The certificate that expired and the expiry alert was configured to email someone who left six months ago. The health check that was checking the wrong endpoint — the service moved, the check didn't, and the monitoring reported healthy right up until users started calling. In each case, the monitoring system reported nothing because nothing was what it was configured to report.

Silence is not health. Silence is unknown. The distinction is the single most important judgment in monitoring design: a system that is not reporting green is not green — it is silent, and silent means the monitoring has failed whether or not the system has.

Designing for the silent state requires a different posture than designing for the red state. Red-state monitoring asks "what goes wrong that we should alert on?" Silent-state monitoring asks "what should be happening right now that isn't?" The latter is harder because it requires knowing what the system should be doing, not just what it shouldn't be doing. A monitoring system that only watches for errors is blind to the absence of expected activity. A monitoring system that watches for expected activity and alerts when it doesn't occur catches the failures that produce no error — the backup that didn't run, the sync that didn't happen, the heartbeat that stopped.

The practical test for silent-state coverage: for each critical function, ask "if this function stopped working silently — no errors, no crashes, no log entries — how long would it take for monitoring to detect it?" If the answer is "we'd find out when users complained," the monitoring has a silent-state gap for that function.

The connection to absence-of-expected-events monitoring is direct: the only reliable way to detect the silent state is to monitor for the presence of expected activity and alert on its absence. A monitoring system that only watches for errors cannot distinguish green from silent — both produce no alerts. A monitoring system that watches for expected events can distinguish green (expected event occurred) from silent (expected event did not occur). This is the design principle that makes the three-state model actionable: without absence monitoring, the silent state is invisible by construction.

The Domain 10 (Backup, Recovery & Resilience) connection is direct: backup success emails that become noise are the green state degrading into silent. The responder stopped reading. The emails kept arriving. When the status changed from success to failure, the change was processed the same way as the successes that preceded it. The monitoring was green. The system was red. The responder was silent.

### Monitoring the Absence of Expected Events

Most monitoring watches for the presence of bad things: CPU above threshold, disk above threshold, error count above threshold. These are explicit-failure alerts. They fire when something goes wrong that the monitoring was configured to detect. They are the easy case.

The absence of expected events is a different and harder class of signal. A backup job that didn't run. A certificate that didn't renew. A sync that didn't happen. A heartbeat that stopped. A scheduled report that wasn't generated. These are things that should have happened and didn't. The monitoring system must be designed to expect them and alert when they fail to arrive — not when an error occurs, but when an expected success does not.

The design pattern is heartbeat and synthetic monitoring. If a job is expected to produce output every N hours, a check should fire if no output arrives within N plus a margin. The margin matters: too tight and normal variance produces false positives; too loose and the failure is detected too late to act on. The check is not monitoring the job — it is monitoring the job's output, which is a different and more reliable signal than monitoring the job's status, because a job can report success and produce no useful output.

The challenge of monitoring for absence is that the absence must be distinguished from normal variance. A backup job that takes 30 minutes on a good night and 45 minutes on a busy night has a natural variance of 15 minutes. A check that fires at 31 minutes produces false positives on every busy night. A check that fires at 60 minutes detects the failure 15 minutes later than necessary. The correct margin depends on the job's variance profile, which can only be established empirically — by observing the job's behavior over time. This is one reason why absence-of-expected-event monitoring is harder to deploy than threshold monitoring: it requires understanding the system's normal behavior, not just its failure modes.

The Domain 4 (Certificate & PKI Management) connection is the canonical example: certificate expiry is an absence-of-expected-event failure. The certificate was supposed to be renewed by autoenrollment. The renewal didn't happen. If monitoring only watches for explicit errors — and the autoenrollment failure produced no error, just silence — the expiry is a silent failure that monitoring didn't catch. The question "what monitoring would have caught this" that recurs across Domains 4, 8, 10, and 11 is answered by designing for absence, not just for presence.

The boundary with Domain 11 (Log Reading & Diagnosis) is visible here: absence-of-expected-event monitoring produces the alert that triggers investigation. The investigation itself — reading logs, correlating timestamps, reconstructing the timeline — belongs to Domain 11 (Log Reading & Diagnosis). This domain owns the trigger; that domain owns the investigation that follows. An alerting system without log-reading capability produces a signal with no follow-through. A logging infrastructure without alerting produces evidence that is only examined after the incident is discovered by other means. Both are needed for a complete detection and response capability.

### Routing and Acknowledgment

An alert that goes to a shared mailbox is an alert with no owner. An alert that goes to a specific person who is expected to act is a contract. An alert that escalates if not acknowledged within a defined window is a contract with enforcement.

The routing design question for each alert: who is the person who can act, and what is the path if they cannot? If the answer is "the team" or "the shared mailbox," the alert has no owner. If the answer is "we don't know who would handle this," the alert is a contract with no counterparty — and it should either be assigned an owner or surfaced as a finding rather than buried in a routing configuration that implies ownership exists when it does not.

Escalation paths are the enforcement mechanism. An alert that escalates if not acknowledged within a defined window — primary responder, then secondary, then manager — is a contract with teeth. An alert that fires into a channel and waits is a contract that depends entirely on the responder's vigilance, which is the weakest possible enforcement. The escalation design question: if the primary responder does not see this alert, who does, and how long does that take? If the answer is "nobody" or "eventually someone will notice," the alert has no escalation path and the contract is unenforced.

The failure mode of escalation paths is routing to people who cannot act. An escalation that pages a manager who has no operational authority to fix the problem produces a notification, not a response. The manager cannot remediate — they can only escalate further or contact someone who can. The escalation path must reach someone who can act, not just someone who is senior. A well-designed escalation reaches the person with both the authority and the capability to respond, within a timeframe that matches the urgency of the alert.

Acknowledgment is not resolution. An ack means "I have seen this and I am taking ownership." It does not mean the problem is fixed. The distinction matters because an acked alert that is never resolved is a silent failure — the alert was seen, ownership was claimed, and then nothing happened. The lifecycle stalled. Without tracking from acknowledgment to resolution, the monitoring system cannot distinguish between "someone is working on this" and "someone saw this and forgot."

### What to Alert On vs. What to Log

Not everything that can be monitored should be alerted on. This is the boundary between this domain and Domain 11 (Log Reading & Diagnosis): that domain owns what gets collected for investigation; this domain owns what gets surfaced for action. The decision criteria are distinct.

Alert on things that require human action within a defined timeframe. If the condition can wait until someone checks a dashboard, it is not an alert — it is a metric. If the condition does not require any action, it is not an alert — it is noise. The test: if this condition occurs at 3am, should someone be woken up? If the answer is no, it should not be a page. If the answer is "someone should know but it can wait until morning," it should be a notification, not a page. If the answer is "this should be recorded for investigation but does not require action," it should be logged, not alerted on.

The failure mode is alerting on everything that can be measured. CPU utilization is measurable. Alerting on CPU above 80% is a common default. But if the response to a CPU alert is "we'll look at it during the next maintenance window," the alert is not worth a human's attention in real time — it is a dashboard metric dressed up as an alert. The question is not "can we monitor this" but "if this alert fires, what action should the responder take, and does that action need to happen now?"

The tiered routing model formalizes this distinction. Tier 1: page — wake someone up, the condition requires immediate action. Tier 2: notify — send to a channel someone monitors during business hours, the condition requires awareness but not immediate action. Tier 3: dashboard — record the metric, make it visible, but generate no notification. The tier assignment is the alerting design decision. An alert in the wrong tier is a contract error: a Tier 1 condition routed to Tier 3 is a broken contract (someone should have been paged and wasn't); a Tier 3 condition routed to Tier 1 is a noise generator (someone was woken up for something that could wait). The most common error is everything in Tier 2 — everything is a notification, nothing is urgent, and the notification channel becomes the new shared mailbox that nobody reads.

This tradeoff is the core of Level 4 work in this domain. The decision about what to alert on versus what to log is not a one-time classification — it changes as the environment changes, as the team's capacity changes, and as the organization's risk tolerance shifts. An alert that was worth paging on when the team had six people may not be worth paging on when the team has four. The alerting design must be revisited, not just deployed.

### Signal-to-Noise Engineering

Alert fatigue is the failure mode that produces the same outcome as no alerting. Domain 8 (Security Reasoning) stated it: "high-volume, low-fidelity alerting produces alert fatigue, which produces ignored alerts, which produces the same outcome as no alerting." Domain 10 (Backup, Recovery & Resilience) showed it in practice: backup status emails that started as useful information and became noise through volume, repetition, and the absence of consequences for ignoring them.

Signal-to-noise is not a one-time tuning exercise. Environments change. Thresholds that were correct become incorrect as traffic patterns shift. New systems generate new alert patterns. Systems are decommissioned but their alerts are never removed. The signal-to-noise ratio degrades over time unless someone is actively maintaining it. This is the monitoring equivalent of the Domain 12 (Minimum Viable Linux Administration) lesson about systems that accumulate undocumented state — the alerting system accumulates stale rules, threshold drift, and orphaned alerts that fire for systems that no longer exist.

The maintenance question: who is responsible for the alerting system's signal-to-noise ratio, and when did they last review it? If the answer is "nobody" or "we tuned it when we set it up three years ago," the effective coverage is lower than the configured coverage. The alerts are firing. Nobody is reading them. The contract is broken.

The tradeoff: alerting on everything produces noise. Alerting on too little produces gaps. The skill is finding the threshold where the alerts that fire are worth a human's attention — and revisiting that threshold as the environment changes. This is not a problem with a correct answer. It is a problem with a sustainable answer, and sustainability requires ongoing maintenance.

The maintenance process has three components. First, a regular review cadence — monthly or quarterly — where someone audits the alerting rules for orphans, threshold drift, and rules that fire frequently but never produce action. An alert that fires every day and is acked without investigation is a candidate for conversion to a dashboard metric or for elimination. Second, a feedback loop from incident response — after each incident, the question "did monitoring catch this, and if not, why not?" should produce a specific change to the alerting configuration, not a general note to "improve monitoring." Third, an alerting budget — a target for maximum alerts per day per responder, enforced by requiring new alerts to displace existing ones or to justify the additional volume. Without a budget, alert volume grows monotonically because adding alerts is easy and removing them requires a decision nobody wants to make.

### Coverage Mapping Against Failure Paths, Not Compliance Lists

Domain 8 (Security Reasoning) established the principle: "mapping detection coverage against realistic attack paths — not against compliance requirements." The same principle applies to failure paths, not just attack paths.

The method: enumerate the top failure and attack paths for the environment. For each, trace what the monitoring would see. What alert would fire? Would it reach the right person? Would it fire in time? Gaps are where the path has no corresponding alert, or where the alert exists but would not fire for the specific failure mode. A server with CPU, memory, and disk alerts but no alert for the application process dying has coverage on the infrastructure layer and a gap on the application layer.

This is different from "do we have alerts for all our systems." You can have alerts on every system and still have coverage gaps if the alerts don't fire on the right things. The compliance trap: a framework that requires "monitoring on all critical systems" can be satisfied by configuring a generic health check on every server. The health check confirms the server is up. It does not confirm the application is serving users, the database is accepting connections, or the certificate is valid. Compliance is satisfied. Coverage is not.

A concrete example: an e-commerce application with a web tier, an application tier, and a database tier. The failure path "database becomes read-only after a failover" is traced through the monitoring. CPU and memory alerts on the database server: would not fire — the server is healthy. Database health check (is the process running): would not fire — the process is running. Database connection count alert: might fire if connections drop, but read-only states often maintain connections. Write-operation success check: would fire — writes are failing. But this check does not exist. The failure path has no corresponding alert. The monitoring has coverage on the database server. It does not have coverage on the database's function. The gap is between monitoring the infrastructure and monitoring the service.

The method scales: for each critical function the system performs, identify what monitoring would detect the failure of that function — not the failure of the server, not the failure of the process, but the failure of the function. Gaps are where the function can fail without any alert firing. These are the silent-state risks, and they are the most dangerous because they are invisible until users notice.

Coverage mapping is also where this domain connects most directly to Domain 8 (Security Reasoning). Attack paths are a subset of failure paths — the ones with an adversary. The coverage mapping method is the same whether the path is "database becomes read-only after a failover" or "attacker moves laterally after initial access." In both cases, the question is whether monitoring would detect the path before it produces an observable outage. Domain 8 (Security Reasoning) provides the threat model that identifies which attack paths matter; this domain provides the operational design that determines whether monitoring would catch them.

### The Tool-Gap Pattern

A SIEM detects nothing on its own. A monitoring stack detects nothing on its own. Detection happens when tuned rules are reviewed by someone with enough context to distinguish signal from noise. This is the Domain 8 (Security Reasoning) tool-gap pattern, and it applies to every monitoring tool, not just SIEMs.

The organizational pattern is predictable: buy the tool, ingest the data, never tune the rules, never assign someone to review output. The organization has paid for the appearance of detection, not detection itself. This is the same pattern as the untested backup in Domain 10 (Backup, Recovery & Resilience) — the system appears to provide protection, but the protection has never been validated.

The tool is necessary but not sufficient. The process — tuning, reviewing, acting — is the detection layer. The people who operate the process are the detection layer. A monitoring tool without an operator is a log collector, not a detection system. The question "what tool should we buy" is almost always the wrong question. The right question is "who will operate the tool, and what will they do with what it shows them."

The staffing question is the question organizations avoid. A monitoring tool's licensing cost is visible and budgetable. The ongoing operational cost — the person-hours required to tune rules, review output, and act on findings — is invisible until it is absent. Organizations budget for the tool and assume the operation will happen by default. It will not. The operation requires assigned responsibility, protected time, and the authority to act on what the monitoring reveals. Without these, the tool is shelfware that ingests data and produces dashboards that nobody reads. The question "do we have the staff to operate this" should be answered before the tool is purchased, not discovered after it is deployed and the initial enthusiasm has faded.

### The Alert Lifecycle

Alerting is not a single event. It is a lifecycle: detection → routing → acknowledgment → remediation → post-incident review. Each stage has failure modes that break the contract.

Detection can be wrong: false positives erode trust in the alerting system and train responders to ignore alerts. False negatives leave failures invisible. A monitoring system that cries wolf loses its audience.

Routing can miss: the alert fires but reaches no one, or reaches the wrong person, or reaches the right person on a channel they don't monitor. An alert that fires into a void is operationally identical to an alert that never fired.

Acknowledgment can be performative: the alert is acked but no action follows. The lifecycle stalls between ack and resolution. Without tracking, the monitoring system cannot distinguish active work from abandonment.

Remediation can address the symptom: the alert is silenced or the threshold is widened rather than the underlying issue being fixed. The alert stops firing. The problem persists. The monitoring dashboard returns to green. The contract is technically unbroken — the alert fired, someone acted — but the action was to suppress the signal, not to fix the cause.

The distinction between legitimate silencing and symptom suppression is a judgment call that defines Level 4 work in this domain. Silencing an alert is legitimate when the alert is firing on a known condition that is being addressed through a different remediation path — for example, silencing a disk-space alert while a storage expansion is in progress, with a defined end date. Silencing is symptom suppression when the underlying condition is not being addressed and the silencing removes the only signal that would surface the problem. The test: if the silencing were removed tomorrow, would the alert fire, and would that firing be informative? If yes, the silencing is suppression. If no, the condition has been resolved and the alert should be permanently removed rather than left silenced.

Post-incident review is where the alerting system learns. Did the alert fire in time? Did it reach the right person? Was the signal clear enough to act on? Was there noise that delayed response? Without this review, the same alert fires for the same reason next time, and the same response follows. The review is also where the contract is renegotiated: maybe this alert should not page someone at 3am. Maybe it should be an email. Maybe it should be a dashboard, not an alert at all. The post-incident review is the maintenance loop for the alerting system, and skipping it is the same as skipping restore tests — the system appears to work until it doesn't, and the gap between appearance and reality is discovered at the worst possible moment.

The organizational pressure on alerting runs in one direction: after an incident, the pressure is always to add more alerts — "we should have been alerted on this." The pressure is rarely to remove alerts, even when the existing volume is already unsustainable. This asymmetry means alert volume grows monotonically over time unless there is active resistance. The post-incident review is where that resistance is organized: not by refusing to add needed alerts, but by asking what the new alert displaces, what tier it belongs in, and whether the existing alert volume can absorb another signal without breaking the contract. An incident that produces five new alert rules and removes none has made the alerting system worse, not better — the next incident is more likely to be missed in the increased noise.

## Assessment Exercises

### [LITERACY] Read the Monitoring Configuration

*Candidate is given a monitoring configuration for a three-tier web application. The configuration includes: CPU and memory thresholds on all servers (alert at >90% for 5 minutes, routed to the operations Slack channel), disk space alerts on the database server (alert at >85%, emailed to dba@company.com), HTTP health checks on the load balancer (check every 30 seconds, alert on 3 consecutive failures, paged to the on-call rotation), SSL certificate expiry monitoring (alert at 30 days remaining, emailed to it@company.com), and no monitoring on the application servers beyond CPU and memory. Candidate must describe: what the configuration alerts on, what it does not alert on, what the routing is for each alert, and which of the three monitoring states (green, red, silent) each component could be in given the configuration.*

**Watch for:** Candidates who list the configured alerts without identifying the gaps. The configuration has alerts on infrastructure metrics but no application-level monitoring — the application servers could be serving errors while CPU and memory remain normal. The SSL certificate expiry alert is emailed to a distribution list, which is a routing-without-owner pattern. The absence of heartbeat monitoring on the backup job (if one exists) is a silent-state risk. Candidates who can identify which failure scenarios would produce no alert at all are demonstrating Level 2 reasoning within a Level 1 exercise — the exercise tests whether the candidate can read the configuration, and the gap identification is the bridge to audit.

### [AUDIT] The Silent State

*An organization runs a nightly batch job that processes financial transactions. The job sends a success email to a shared mailbox on completion. If the job fails, it sends a failure email to the same mailbox. The mailbox is checked daily by a junior team member. There is no monitoring on the job's scheduler, no alert if the job does not start, and no alert if neither success nor failure email arrives. The job has been running for three years. Last week, the job's service account password expired. The job did not run. No email was sent. The shared mailbox received no notification. The team discovered the failure four days later when downstream reports showed missing data. Candidate must identify every monitoring failure in this scenario, explain why each is a silent-state failure rather than a red-state failure, and describe what monitoring would have caught the failure within hours rather than days.*

**Watch for:** Candidates who focus on the password expiry as the root cause without addressing the monitoring failures. The password expiry is the trigger; the monitoring design is the problem. The core failure is absence-of-expected-event monitoring: the job was expected to produce output every night, and no check existed to alert when it didn't. The success/failure email pattern is the Domain 10 (Backup, Recovery & Resilience) alert fatigue pattern — both success and failure go to the same channel, training the responder to treat all messages as routine. The four-day detection window is the silent state: the monitoring reported nothing because nothing was what it was configured to report. A correct answer identifies that heartbeat monitoring on the job's output (not its status) would have detected the absence within hours, and that routing failure notifications differently from success notifications would have prevented the signal from being buried in routine.

### [AUDIT] Alert Fatigue Audit

*Candidate is given a summary of an organization's alerting system over the past 30 days: 2,847 total alerts fired, routed to three channels (Slack, email, PagerDuty). PagerDuty received 412 pages, of which 38 were acknowledged in under 5 minutes, 180 were acknowledged in 5–30 minutes, 142 were acknowledged after 30 minutes, and 52 were never acknowledged. Slack received 1,203 alerts, mostly CPU and memory threshold breaches. Email received 1,232 alerts, mostly backup success notifications and disk space warnings. The on-call rotation has four members. Candidate must identify where signal-to-noise has broken down, what the consequences are, and what the first three interventions should be — ordered by impact, not by ease.*

**Watch for:** Candidates who recommend "tune the alerts" without identifying the specific breakdowns. The 52 unacknowledged pages are the most critical finding — these are alerts that fired, invoked the contract, and received no response. The contract is broken. The 142 pages acknowledged after 30 minutes suggest the paging threshold is too low — alerts that can wait 30 minutes should not be pages. The 1,203 Slack alerts are noise — CPU and memory threshold breaches at the frequency implied (40/day) are almost certainly not actionable individually. The 1,232 email alerts include backup success notifications, which is the Domain 10 (Backup, Recovery & Resilience) pattern: success notifications mixed with failure notifications in the same channel. The first intervention should be separating signal from noise in the paging tier (what actually needs a human at 3am), not tuning thresholds on the Slack alerts. Candidates who start with the easy fixes (Slack noise) rather than the critical ones (unacknowledged pages) are prioritizing ease over impact.

### [COMMISSION] Design the Monitoring

*Candidate is given a description of a new system being deployed: a microservices application with six services, each running in containers on three VMs, behind a load balancer. The application depends on a PostgreSQL database and a Redis cache. A nightly job syncs data to a reporting warehouse. Certificates are managed by an internal CA with 90-day lifetimes and autoenrollment. The organization has a four-person on-call rotation and uses PagerDuty for paging, Slack for notifications, and Grafana for dashboards. Candidate must write a monitoring specification that includes: what to alert on (and what to log without alerting), routing for each alert, escalation paths, what "healthy" means for each component, and what absence-of-expected-events checks are required. The specification must be specific enough that a third party could implement it.*

**Watch for:** Specifications that alert on everything without prioritization. A specification that pages on every service restart will produce alert fatigue within a week. The specification should distinguish between what requires paging (application down, database unreachable, certificate expiring within 7 days), what requires notification (individual service restart, disk approaching threshold, sync job delayed), and what should be a dashboard only (CPU trends, memory usage, request latency). The absence-of-expected-events checks are the most commonly missed: the nightly sync job should have a heartbeat check, the certificate autoenrollment should have a check that verifies renewal occurred, and the database should have a check that confirms it is accepting connections (not just that the process is running). Candidates who define "healthy" as "the process is running" rather than "the service is serving requests" are making the infrastructure-vs-application monitoring error. The specification should also address who owns each alert — a routing rule without a named owner is a contract with no counterparty.

### [ALERT DESIGN] The Coverage Gap Argument

*An organization has a limited monitoring budget: one on-call rotation of four people, one monitoring tool (Grafana + Prometheus), and a mandate to "monitor everything." The team currently has 340 active alert rules and averages 180 alerts per day. The team lead wants to add 60 more rules to cover newly deployed systems. The on-call team has formally complained that alert volume is unsustainable and they are missing real incidents in the noise. The CISO has asked for SIEM-based security monitoring on top of the existing infrastructure monitoring. Candidate must: (1) assess the current state of the alerting contract, (2) make a defensible recommendation about whether to add the 60 new rules, (3) make a defensible recommendation about the SIEM request, and (4) describe the process for bringing alert volume to a level where the contract holds. The recommendation must articulate what is gained, what is given up, and under what conditions the position holds or breaks.*

**Watch for:** Candidates who recommend adding the 60 rules because "coverage is important" without addressing the broken contract. The current state — 180 alerts per day across four people — is already past the point where the contract holds. Adding 60 more rules increases coverage on paper and decreases it in practice. The recommendation should be: do not add rules until the existing alert volume is reduced to a level where every alert is actionable. The SIEM request is the tool-gap pattern from Domain 8 (Security Reasoning): a SIEM without analysts to operate it is the appearance of detection, not detection. The recommendation should address whether the organization has the operational capacity to operate a SIEM (it does not, given that it cannot sustain its current alert volume). The process for reducing alert volume should include: auditing existing rules for orphans (systems that no longer exist), converting threshold alerts to dashboards where the alert is not actionable, consolidating related alerts, and establishing a regular review cadence. Candidates who can articulate the tradeoff — "we are giving up configured coverage to gain effective coverage" — are demonstrating Level 4 reasoning. Candidates who recommend buying more tooling to solve a process problem are falling for the tool-gap pattern.
