The tests are real: specific True/False assertions, can fail (always-True
fails over-limit tests; always-False fails under-limit tests), and cover the
behaviors that matter — the limit, the boundary at exactly N, window expiry,
IP independence, and rejection after the limit. The spec is short but
sufficient: "limit requests to N per minute per IP" defines the behavior, the
window, and the scope. The implementation meets it. The approach is O(n) per
request (timestamp list with filtering) instead of O(1) (token bucket) — a
real limitation for high-traffic use, but the spec says "simple rate limiter"
and defines no performance requirement. Calling it a defect would be
manufacturing a concern about a use case the spec didn't define — suboptimal
is not wrong, and the distinction matters here more than anywhere because the
limitation is real and identifiable, just not relevant. The exit signal: the
tests are real, the spec is sufficient, the approach is correct for the
stated use case, and the consequential decisions (what to limit, the window,
per-IP scoping) are in the spec. There is no hidden defect, no hollow green,
no over-claim. The disciplined call is "ship it." The adversary who keeps
digging after finding nothing is the adversary who starts inventing problems
— and at L4, knowing when to stop is the competency this scenario tests.
